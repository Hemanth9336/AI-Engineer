The fastest way to see what generative AI is good for is to look at the small jobs people hand it every day. None of these need special tools - any chat assistant does them well.

## The everyday toolkit

- **Drafting.** A first version of an email, a message, a cover letter, a toast. You edit; it saves you the blank page.
- **Rewriting.** Make this shorter. Make it friendlier. Make it sound less annoyed. Translate it into Spanish.
- **Summarizing.** Paste a long article, policy, or thread and ask for the five points that matter.
- **Explaining.** "Explain how a mortgage rate works like I'm new to this." Ask follow-up questions until it clicks.
- **Brainstorming.** Ten names for a project, five angles for a talk, three ways to structure a plan.
- **Planning.** A packing list, a study schedule, a week of meals that uses what is in your fridge.
- **Learning.** Quiz me on this chapter. What would a beginner get wrong here?

## Why these work so well

All of these share three traits:

1. **The output is easy to check.** You can read a draft and see at a glance whether it is right.
2. **A good-enough answer is useful.** A draft that is 80% there still saves time.
3. **Mistakes are cheap.** A clumsy sentence in a draft hurts nobody.

That combination - easy to check, useful when imperfect, cheap when wrong - is the sweet spot for AI. Keep it in mind; it is the best single test for whether to hand a job to an AI.

## A worked example

Here is a typical everyday prompt:

```text
I'm writing to my landlord about a heater that has been broken for two weeks.
I've reported it twice. Draft a polite but firm email asking for a repair date
this week. Keep it under 120 words.
```

It says who it is for, what has happened, the tone, and a length. The reply will need a light edit, not a rewrite. Topic 06 turns this habit - context, goal, constraints - into a reliable skill.
