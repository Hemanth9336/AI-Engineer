type: lesson
title: "Demoing without overpromising"
minutes: 7
---

A demo is a promise. Whatever the audience sees, they'll expect on day one of production - so show them what's real.

## Before the demo

- **Use their data and their questions.** Ask the customer for five real questions in advance; add two you know are hard.
- **Rehearse with the actual system**, at the actual temperature, on the actual network. Run every question several times - models aren't deterministic.
- **Plan for failure.** Decide in advance what you'll say when an answer is wrong. It will happen.
- **Have a backup**: screenshots or a recording, in case the network or the provider fails.

## During the demo

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Show a trap question and the refusal | Only show questions you know work |
| Click a citation and show the source | Say "it's always right" |
| Say what it doesn't do yet | Imply features you haven't built |
| Show the eval numbers next to the live demo | Let one great answer stand in for quality |
| Let them type a question | Grab the keyboard back when it gets hard |

## The honest frame

Open with what they're seeing: "This is a one-week prototype on 40 pages of your handbook. It answers with citations and says 'I don't know' when the handbook doesn't cover something. On 50 test questions it got 44 right; here are the 6 it got wrong and why."

That framing makes a wrong answer during the demo a **confirmation** of what you said, not a surprise. The most credible demos include a failure that you explain calmly.

## After the demo

Send a short summary: what was shown, the measured results, the known limitations, and the proposed next step. Memories of demos improve over time; a written summary keeps expectations anchored.
