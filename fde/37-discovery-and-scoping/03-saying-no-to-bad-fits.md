Saying yes to every idea is how AI projects fail. Part of an FDE's value is the judgement to say **"not this, not yet - here's what instead."**

## Signs of a bad fit

| Signal | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| **No data** - "the knowledge is in Pieter's head" | Nothing to retrieve, nothing to evaluate |
| **No metric** - "we want to be more innovative" | You can't show it worked |
| **Zero error tolerance, no human in the loop** - "it must never be wrong, and no one checks" | LLMs make mistakes; the design can't absorb them |
| **Better solved without AI** - a form, a search box, a rule engine, a SQL report | Cheaper, faster, more reliable |
| **Wrong kind of AI** - forecasting, optimisation, anomaly detection on numbers | Classical ML or operations research does it better |
| **Political, not practical** - a pet project to impress the board | Low adoption once the demo is over |

## How to say no well

1. **Acknowledge the goal** behind the idea: "You want suppliers to stop waiting days for answers."
2. **Name the specific problem** with this version: "Resolving disputes automatically in SAP means the AI moves money with no review."
3. **Offer a path**: a smaller first step that serves the same goal, and the conditions to revisit the big one.
4. **Put it in writing**: an "out of scope" list with reasons makes the decision durable - and stops it returning every meeting.

## "Not yet" is often the right answer

Many bad-fit ideas become good fits later: after the documents are cleaned up, after the first assistant has proven quality, after a human review step exists. Record them in the scoping document with what would need to be true.
