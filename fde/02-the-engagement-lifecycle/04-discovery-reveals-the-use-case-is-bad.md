title: "Discovery reveals the use case is bad"
minutes: 5
---

The customer asked for an assistant that predicts which shipments will be late. In discovery you learn: delivery data lives in a carrier portal nobody can export from, there are no records of why past shipments were late, and the operations team says late shipments are rare and already handled by phone.

### Q1. What should you do?

- A. Build it anyway - the customer asked for it
- B. Report the findings plainly and propose a better-fitting use case you found during the interviews
- C. Quietly change the use case without telling anyone
- D. End the engagement immediately

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Answer: B** - Saying no early is part of finding the right problem - and you usually find a better use case in the same interviews.

</details>

### Q2. Why is ending this in discovery a success, not a failure?

- A. It isn't - you failed
- B. Finding a bad fit in two weeks costs far less than finding it after a two-month pilot
- C. Discovery doesn't count
- D. The customer will forget

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Answer: B** - Discovery exists to fail cheaply.

</details>

### Q3. Which finding alone would make this use case a bad fit, even with good data?

- A. The model hasn't seen their data
- B. The team uses phones
- C. The carrier portal has a login page
- D. The problem is rare and already handled well, so success can't create real value

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Answer: D** - No real pain means no measurable value - the core test of a use case.

</details>
